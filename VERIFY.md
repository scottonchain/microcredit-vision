## Evidence for "When a guarantee can replenish itself" and "What if $100 could stand behind more than one first job?" (Claude Code, 2026-10-11)

Internal theory, not a result, published by the operator's direction. The design is the project's active candidate, a revolving first-job payment guarantee. Every figure comes from the internal theory draft at microcredit-theory `3626a94` ([DRAFT.md](https://github.com/scottonchain/microcredit-theory/blob/3626a94/revolving-first-hire-guarantee/DRAFT.md)) and its simulation ([ruin.py](https://github.com/scottonchain/microcredit-theory/blob/3626a94/revolving-first-hire-guarantee/ruin.py), output in [results.txt](https://github.com/scottonchain/microcredit-theory/blob/3626a94/revolving-first-hire-guarantee/results.txt): seed 1, 4,000 trials per row, 500 jobs, one open guarantee at a time). Anyone can rerun it with Python 3 and no dependencies.

| Posts say | Checked record and scope |
| --- | --- |
| The reserve changes on average by (1 − p)qr − pG − c per job, and holds level when r = (pG + c) / ((1 − p)q) | Arithmetic: per job a contribution r arrives with probability (1 − p)q, a claim of G is paid with probability p, and the reserve spends c. Draft section 4. |
| For a $60 job that is $1.23 to $9.72, 2% to 16% of the wage | Draft section 4 table: p = 1%, q = 0.9, G = $60, c = $0.50 gives $1.23 (2.1%); p = 10%, q = 0.8, G = $60, c = $1 gives $9.72 (16.2%). |
| A $100 reserve backing $60 guarantees ran out within 500 jobs 39% to 51% of the time, even with contributions half again above break-even | results.txt, "Reserve size": q = 0.8, c = $1, 50% loading, $100 reserve: 0.387 at p = 3%, 0.507 at p = 10%. |
| Capping each guarantee at $20, a fifth of the reserve, brought that down to between 0.3% and 3% | results.txt, "Correlation" rows with rho = 0 (same q = 0.8, c = $1, 50% loading, $100 reserve, G = $20): 0.003 at p = 3%, 0.030 at p = 10%. |
| One client failing several workers at once raised it sharply | results.txt, "Correlation": blocks of five jobs sharing one client's outcome with probability 0.2 or 0.5 raise ruin at p = 3% from 0.3% to 14.9% and 20.1%. |
| A $100 reserve should back only small guarantees, about $20 a job, one job per client at a time, and should pause when it runs low | Draft sections 5 and 9, and the project's north star: the guarantee at most a fifth of the reserve, one open guarantee per client, no new guarantee while the reserve is below twice the guarantee. |
| Nothing is lent; it is closer to mutual insurance, with the premium taken only from workers who were paid | Draft section 7: no money moves to the worker when the client pays and nothing is repaid after a claim; the contribution is collected at payment or treated as a pledge never pursued. |
| Large freelance platforms already hold a client's money in escrow before fixed-price work starts | Third-party guides to Upwork's payment protection read through search results on 2026-10-11 (for example gigradar.io and aiproposer.com): the client funds a fixed-price milestone before work starts; unfunded work is not protected. Upwork's own help pages were not read directly. |
| The barrier newcomers face there is being hired at all | Pallais, NBER working paper w18917, as in the evidence for "A dollar that backs a first job" below. |
| No reserve can be promised to last for ever | Draft section 5: a finite reserve has a positive chance of running out on a bad streak at any loading; a $100 reserve backing a $60 guarantee stops for good if its first claim comes before its first contribution, with probability p / (p + (1 − p)q). |

The working paper was read in full by Hermes (an AI agent on the team) on 2026-10-11; the published version was not read. | In the oDesk experiment, 18% of the people hired never returned the work | Same paper and reading: 18% of treated workers never returned the file and were not rated, so the treatment is an offer of a job. Using that share as a failure rate for a guarantee is the post's assumption, labelled as such: it counts workers who did not deliver in 2010, not work a client rejected. |
| At an 18% failure rate accepted wages would be about 4.6 times the refunds, at 30% about 2.3 times; that is an upper bound, not the benefit | Arithmetic. With a share f of first jobs failing and each failure refunded in full, wages for accepted work divided by refunds is (1 − f) / f: 0.82 / 0.18 = 4.56; 0.7 / 0.3 = 2.33. It counts every guaranteed hire as caused by the guarantee and omits fees, support, disputes and displacement, so it bounds the benefit from above. (Revised 2026-10-11 16:45 UTC; the first version also gave 4 to 1 at a fifth and 9 to 1 at 10%, and called the division the measure.) |
| The real measure divides the extra spendable earnings the guarantee caused, minus work it took from others, by everything the sponsor spends; a random half of jobs shows what it caused | The project's rule for any route that asks for sponsor money: benefit counts only what the route causes beyond what would have happened anyway, measured against a comparison group, net of the beneficiary's costs ([north star](https://github.com/scottonchain/microcredit-agent-testbed/blob/main/coordination/NORTH_STAR.md)). The correction follows two internal reviews of the first version: Codex's one-edit review (contract issue 7, comment 6111202565) and an independent Claude Code check (testbed PR 53, comment 6111219156). A rough form, with a the share of hires caused and s the worker's net share of the wage: because the client pays every wage and a failed newcomer keeps it, benefit per sponsor dollar before costs is a × s / f + (1 − a), about 1.9 at f = 18% and a = s = 0.5 (an earlier version of this row gave a × s × (1 − f) / f, about 1.1, which omitted failed workers' pay; corrected by the same reviewer, testbed PR 53 comment 6111427856). Randomising by application, as the first revision said, would mostly measure applicants displacing each other, so the post now says jobs (revised 2026-10-11 17:23 UTC). |

## Where the design falls short

The honest list is [CREDIT_INTEGRITY_ISSUES.md](https://github.com/scottonchain/microcredit-contract/blob/main/docs/CREDIT_INTEGRITY_ISSUES.md). The open items that matter most for the README's story:

- **Cold start.** A stranger with no history gets nothing until an issuer grants a line, someone backs them, or they stake. That is the price of the invariant, and the next problem to solve.
- **The issuer is the trust root.** Only the issuer (an oracle) and the owner create unsecured credit. The issuer's total is budgeted, but it does not yet put capital behind its lines (CI-17).
- **One price for every loan.** Stake-secured loans pay the same premium as unsecured ones (CI-18).
- **One hop.** Received backing cannot be passed on. That halves liquidity on simulated networks but keeps every loss on an account that chose the borrower (CI-20).

## For a lender: what is bounded, and what is not

A lender's loss is bounded by Theorem 2: realised plus potential loss never exceeds the credit lines issued plus the dues borrowers paid. Defaults are charged first to the backers' stake, then to their credit, then to a first-loss reserve, and only then to lenders. What is not bounded is the issuer's judgement: if it grants lines to bad borrowers, lenders bear what the reserve does not. That is why the issuer's budget and policy, not the pool's code, decide whether a stranger's loan is worth funding.
